For more than a decade, LinkedIn was part of my working infrastructure.

I used it to speak with founders, senior professionals, candidates and people across India’s startup and recruitment ecosystem. Over time, that activity produced roughly 25,000 followers and a professional identity that was strongly associated with my LinkedIn profile.

Then the account disappeared.

LinkedIn initially described the restriction as temporary and said it had detected signs of possible unauthorised access. I was asked to verify my identity, so I submitted a government-issued ID.

After verification, the explanation changed. The Trust and Consumer Support team informed me that the account would remain permanently restricted for alleged violations of LinkedIn’s User Agreement and Professional Community Policies.

They would not tell me:

- which activity violated the policy;
- the broad policy category involved;
- when the alleged activity occurred;
- whether it related to content, messaging, invitations, account access or automation;
- whether a security incident could have caused it; or
- what corrective action could prevent recurrence.

The stated reason for withholding this information was privacy.

## The most plausible trigger

Before the restriction, I had experimented with browser-based AI automation around LinkedIn workflows.

The objective was straightforward: reduce repetitive work around professional engagement. Browser automation can read what a person sees on a screen and help draft or perform actions where an official API does not provide access.

I knew this carried policy risk. LinkedIn’s official interfaces do not expose many messaging and connection workflows, and automating the browser can violate platform rules even when the underlying intent is not malicious.

Was that the reason for the restriction?

I cannot state that as fact because LinkedIn has never confirmed it. It remains the most plausible explanation available to me. The honest formulation is:

> My account was permanently restricted after I used browser-based automation, but LinkedIn refused to identify whether automation was the trigger.

That distinction matters. This is not an attempt to rewrite a known violation as an unexplained accident. It is an account of taking a known platform risk and then confronting an enforcement process that provided no intelligible allegation.

## What I got wrong

The easy reaction would be to focus entirely on LinkedIn’s process. That would avoid my own responsibility.

I understood that browser automation could lead to an account restriction. I had previously considered the difference between an official API and unofficial browser access. I knew platforms detect repetitive or machine-like behaviour. I knew that convenience did not create permission.

But I treated the risk as a technical compliance issue rather than a balance-sheet issue.

That was the mistake.

The downside was not the loss of a login. The real exposure included:

- more than a decade of professional history;
- approximately 25,000 followers;
- access to conversations and relationships;
- a search result that had become part of my identity;
- a reliable channel for reaching candidates and founders; and
- the accumulated credibility attached to one URL.

When the possible downside is that large, “the automation saves time” is a weak justification.

## What LinkedIn got wrong

Platforms need strong safety systems. They should detect compromised accounts, abusive outreach, scraping and manipulation. They should not publish the details of internal detection systems.

But protecting an investigation is different from withholding the nature of an allegation.

An affected member should at least be told whether the concern relates broadly to:

- account security;
- content;
- invitations;
- messaging;
- automation;
- account sharing; or
- another policy category.

Without that information, a member cannot meaningfully appeal, secure the account, remove an integration or prevent recurrence.

In my case, the first communication treated the matter as a possible security incident. The final communication treated it as a confirmed policy violation. Identity was verified, but the allegation remained undisclosed.

That is not a usable appeal process.

## A professional platform is infrastructure

LinkedIn is often described as social media. For recruiters, founders and job seekers, that description is incomplete.

It functions as professional infrastructure.

Hiring pipelines, reputations, business-development conversations and years of published knowledge can all accumulate inside one platform. The platform can then remove access unilaterally, based on rules it interprets and an appeal process it controls.

That may be contractually permissible. It is still strategically dangerous for the user.

## The lessons I am carrying forward

### 1. Do not automate a channel you cannot afford to lose

Before automating any platform, calculate the full value at risk. Followers are only one component. Include history, relationships, discoverability and business continuity.

### 2. Separate assistance from execution

AI can research, summarise and draft outside a platform. The final action can remain manual. That loses some efficiency but materially reduces platform risk.

### 3. Build on a domain you control

My permanent professional identity is now `varunjain.info`. Social accounts will distribute the work, but the canonical copy will live here.

### 4. Convert attention into portable relationships

RSS, email subscribers and direct relationships are transferable. Platform followers are not.

### 5. Keep evidence and recovery paths

Maintain exports where platforms allow them. Preserve original articles outside the platform. Use two-factor authentication, unique passwords and session reviews.

### 6. Never let one platform become your identity

This is the deepest lesson. I had not merely built an audience on LinkedIn. I had allowed LinkedIn to become the primary container for my professional identity.

The account restriction exposed that dependency in one day.

## What happens next

I am still pursuing a fair review. I am not asking LinkedIn to reveal detection methods or provide a policy exception. I am asking for the broad allegation, an independent manual review and an opportunity to correct any non-compliant integration or security issue.

But the larger decision no longer depends on whether the account returns.

My writing, guides, research and professional history will now live on infrastructure I control. LinkedIn, X and any future platform can help people discover that work. None of them will be allowed to remain its only home.

Losing 25,000 followers is expensive.

Failing to learn from it would be worse.
